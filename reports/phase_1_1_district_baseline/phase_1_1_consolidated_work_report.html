1. Goal And Research Frame

Goal Of Phase 1.1

Phase 1.1 establishes a clean, national, district-level discovery baseline for likely Shiva temple candidates in India. The purpose is to create a comparable administrative starting point before adding towns, urban local bodies, sub-districts, or villages.

The working research question is: using official district geography and a bounded Google Places discovery protocol, where do Shiva-related temple candidates appear, and how strong is the name-based Shiva signal?

Why District-Only First

  • Districts are official, recognizable units for research and government communication.
  • A district layer prevents early mixing of towns, ULBs, tehsils, villages, and legacy pilot rows.
  • The task volume is controlled: 785 districts times 9 keywords.
  • It creates a national benchmark before deeper local expansion.

What This Work Can Support

  • District-level prioritization for manual review.
  • Evidence packs for academic or government conversations.
  • A repeatable pipeline for later location layers.
  • An audit trail for administrative naming decisions.

District Names

The district layer uses Local Government Directory data as the administrative reference. The Ministry of Panchayati Raj describes LGD as a directory for land/revenue regions and local governments, with unique LGD codes assigned to government bodies and administrative entities.

https://panchayat.gov.in/en/lgd/

Temple Names

There is no final temple-name source of truth in Phase 1.1. Google Places names are treated as discovered field signals. A future verification phase should triangulate candidates against temple trusts, state religious/endowment departments, gazetteers, tourism boards, ASI/state archaeology sources, local records, and manual review.

4. Discovery Protocol

Search Design

  • Each active district received the same keyword set for comparability.
  • Queries used the pattern: keyword in district, state, India.
  • The run used Google Places Text Search with includedType: hindu_temple, strictTypeFiltering: true, regionCode: IN, and languageCode: en.
  • Fields were limited to the needed result attributes, including ID, display name, address, location, type, and Maps URI.

5. Deduplication And Classification

Dedupe Method

Within each API response, repeated Google Place IDs were collapsed. During database writes, candidates were upserted by google_place_id, so rediscovery through another query updated the same candidate instead of creating a second candidate row.

This is an operational dedupe method. It avoids repeated API hits becoming repeated candidate records, but it does not prove canonical cultural identity where Google itself has split or merged place records.

Classification Method

Candidate confidence is based on the discovered name only. High-confidence terms include direct Shiva variants such as Shiva, Shiv, Mahadev, Vishwanath, Somnath, Kedarnath, Omkareshwar, Lingeshwar, linga, and lingam. Medium-confidence terms include Shankar, Ishwar/Eshwar, Nath, Rudra, Someshwar, and Bholenath.

Low confidence means no configured Shiva-specific term matched the discovered name. It does not mean the candidate is not a Shiva temple.

7. Challenges And Research Controls

Challenges Encountered

  • Some downloaded files were not what their filenames suggested. One "towns" file was actually Uttar Pradesh sub-district data.
  • LGD local body data was valuable, but mixing it into Phase 1.1 would have blurred the district baseline.
  • Administrative names vary across sources: spelling variants, renames, parenthetical labels, and legacy district names needed careful alignment.
  • Two Assam districts were present in LGD but missing in the database, while one Arunachal pilot row sat outside the LGD district baseline.
  • Google Places returns discovery evidence, not a canonical temple registry, so counts must remain uncertainty-aware.
  • Repeated discoveries across keywords created heavy duplication, making stable place-ID dedupe essential.

Controls Applied

  • Phase 1.1 was explicitly restricted to active district rows.
  • LGD district codes were preserved as administrative identifiers.
  • Alignment output was written to review CSVs before applying changes.
  • Manual exceptions were documented and retained in the audit trail.
  • Google Places calls used bounded limits and a narrow field mask.
  • Reports state discovery counts rather than final real-world temple counts.

Known Limitations

  • Candidate names are Google Places display names, not verified canonical temple names.
  • Some real temples may not be in Google Places, may be typed differently, or may not match the selected keywords.
  • Some candidates may be non-Shiva temples whose names contain overlapping terms, or Shiva temples whose names do not contain configured terms.
  • The candidate-discovery event ledger now records query-to-place attribution. Historical backfill captures each candidate's latest known source attribution; future discovery runs record new observations automatically.

8. Path Forward

Immediate Next Research Steps

  • Rerun or inspect the one failed district task and document the outcome.
  • Sample high, medium, and low confidence candidates across top and low-volume districts.
  • Use the candidate-discovery event ledger to analyze repeated discoveries across keywords, districts, towns, and future location layers.
  • Create a manual review protocol with fields for canonical name, deity verification, source URL, verifier, and confidence notes.
  • Keep Phase 1.2 town and ULB expansion as a separate scoped report, not mixed into Phase 1.1.

Suggested External Positioning

Use: An uncertainty-aware district-level discovery baseline of likely Shiva temple candidates in India, generated from official Indian district data and Google Places discovery results.

Avoid: exact number of Shiva temples, official census of temples, complete temple database.
